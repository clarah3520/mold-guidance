Communication and accessibility

Source Effective communication? (language & readability) Multiple situations / circumstances covered? Accessible & doable by everyone? (equity: ability, cost, tools, literacy, language) Additional resources / referrals for those who can't
U.S. EPA Plain, conversational, homeowner-friendly - short sentences, photos, a bathroom tip. Minor flaw: technical asides (respirator fit-testing, ACGIH) and two overlapping pages (basic steps vs brief guide). Covers owners and renters, HVAC, hidden mold, sewage/contaminated water and small-vs-large jobs. Gap: nothing for people who can't do the work themselves - no disability, low-income or 'no-one to help' scenario. Assumes an able-bodied person can buy PPE (quotes N95 at $12-25), a humidity meter and a dehumidifier, and do the work. No accommodation for disability, cost or physical limits. Strong language range (ES + ~11). Points to the schools/commercial guide, ACGIH and to vetting contractors; tells renters to contact local/state/federal health or housing authorities. Flaw: 'phone books' is dated; no financial-assistance pointers.
U.S. CDC Very readable - 'key points', bold reminders, a clear 'who shouldn't clean' list. Flaw: so brief there's little method detail, and the bleach nuance (when NOT to) sits on a separate linked PDF. Strong on crises - flooding, community-wide events, staying in a moldy home for weeks. Gap: little on everyday small mold, no owner/renter distinction, nothing on cost or disability. Tells high-risk people not to clean or even stay - but never says who cleans for someone with no alternative. Assumes PPE access. Good language range (ES + ~15). Links a resources page and 'reducing exposure' / 'when to use bleach' PDFs. Flaw: thin on who-to-call for people who can't clean; no professional-vetting or financial referral.
Health Canada Clear, well-structured six-step format. Flaw: 16 pages is long for quick use; terms like 'efflorescence' and 'non-viable air test' go unexplained; dense in places. Broadest situational coverage - owners, landlords, tenants, flooding, cannabis cultivation, climate change, overcrowding, hidden mold, room-by-room. Gap: still assumes the reader (or someone) is able and funded to act. Says susceptible people should stay away from cleanup but assumes another able person is available, and that funds exist for a professional on large jobs. PPE cost unaddressed. Only EN/FR. Strong: a full 'hiring a professional' section (how to vet, IICRC/OSHA training, ISO-17025 labs), a landlord/tenant pointer to provincial rules, and a contact phone/email.
CCOHS Organized by area size, but an occupational register with unexplained jargon (APR, PAPR, P100, HEPA). Higher reading level than the consumer guides. Workplace scenarios, HVAC, sanitary vs unsanitary water, three size tiers. Gap: no home, renter or vulnerable-household situations. Written for workplaces where the employer supplies PPE and training; for an individual it assumes the same equipment and know-how. EN/FR only. Points to Health Canada, NYC and the Canadian Construction Association, plus a Safety InfoLine phone. Referrals are workplace-oriented, not for households who can't cope.
U.S. OSHA Bureaucratic and technical - cites CFR standards and Level I-IV categories; not written for a lay reader. Workplace only, organized by contamination size. Gap: no homes, renters, vulnerable occupants, cost or disability. Assumes employer-provided, fit-tested respirators and trained personnel - not something an individual can access or do. EN + ES. Refers to EPA/NYC guides and industrial-hygiene consultants. Little for individuals who can't do the work; advisory only.
City of Toronto A policy/legal register (roles, responsibilities, authorities), not instructional prose. Hard for a layperson to act on; methods sit in appendices. Covers City-owned/leased and multi-tenant sites, sensitive settings (long-term care, childcare), leased-vs-owned and procurement. Gap: only City property - nothing for the general public. Accessible within its scope (the City supplies training/PPE to staff) but irrelevant to a resident. EN only plus a translate widget. Internal referrals only - qualified persons, consultants, OHS staff. No public-facing help or financial pathways.
Ottawa Public Health Friendly Q&A, plain language - effective for the public. Flaw: the page is one long set of dense tables, and content largely repeats Health Canada. Owners, tenants and landlords (with recourse), a flooding link and hidden-mold advice. Gap: assumes the reader is physically able to do the cleanup. Assumes DIY ability and PPE purchase. Wide language reach, but many languages are via Google machine translation rather than full translated documents. Best referral pathways for those who can't fix it themselves: notify landlord, then 3-1-1 / Bylaw Property Standards and the Landlord & Tenant Board (with phone), plus HC/CMHC/CDC links and numbers.
HealthLink BC The plainest and shortest - very easy to read. Flaw: brevity omits the 'why' and most method detail; says 'KN-95' where others say N95. General home guidance and an asthma/humidifier caution, folded into a broader biological-contaminants file. Gap: no owner/renter distinction, no flooding depth, no equity angle. Assumes DIY ability and PPE. Strongest language access of all (9 languages) but no accommodation for disability or cost. Good referrals: Health Canada, BC Lung, CMHC (incl. a First-Nations occupants guide), the Lung Association, and an 8-1-1 nurse line offering translation in ~130 languages.
AIHA Professional/technical - 'IEQ', 'OEHS', risk-communication framing; written for practitioners, not the public. Frames large-building and disaster-response contexts. Gap: not situational for households at all. The authoritative 'Green Book' is paywalled and the guidance presumes a qualified consultant - not publicly doable. EN + some ES fact sheets. Offers a consultants directory and links to CDC/FEMA/EPA/OSHA/CCOHS. These are professional referrals, not help for a resident who can't act.
NIH (DOHS) SOP Formal SOP, acronym-heavy (DOHS, ORF, AHU, MVOC) and legalistic - not for a general reader. Institutional and healthcare-specific - patient-care rules, contaminated water, off-hours scheduling. Gap: none of it maps to a household. Requires DOHS staff/contractors and specialized gear (moisture meters, FLIR, HEPA, air-injection) - not individually doable; internal document, EN only. Internal contacts only (DOHS, Occupational Medical Service, a Call Desk). No public or financial referrals.
OHSCO / PSHSA Workplace tone with scientific names (Stachybotrys, Aspergillus); moderate reading level, largely a walkthrough of the EPA chart. Workplace scenarios and size tiers, including sewage. Gap: narrow - no homes, renters or vulnerable households. Assumes an employer/worker context with PPE and training. EN only; many linked resources are dead legacy URLs. Lists agency publications (EPA, Health Canada, NYC, IRSST, AIHA, Manitoba) - but several links are broken and it targets employers, not individuals.
NCCEH Academic evidence-review register with inline citations - written for public-health inspectors, not the public. Home-focused in intent but explicitly EXCLUDES flooding, hidden mold and grow-ops - it skips the hardest common situations; notes bigger buildings need more. Presumes professional involvement beyond small areas; no equity, cost or disability angle. EN (FR via a sister centre). Includes a 'recommended resources for homeowners' list (CMHC, Health Canada, NIEHS, CDC, EPA). Flaw: a 2010 document, so several links are dated.

Professional mold remediation

Industry practice and recommendations: the standards remediators follow, what happens on a job, add-on treatments, what companies tell homeowners, and regulation in Ontario.

Standards remediators follow

Document Who writes it / audience How jobs are sized Biocides and coatings Verification
ANSI/IICRC S520-2024 (4th ed., replaced 2015) Industry consensus, IICRC; remediators, insurers, courts treat it as "standard of care" By condition: 1-normal, 2-settled spores, 3-actual growth; not square footage Strongly discourages spraying without first removing mold; ozone and antimicrobial devices not primary tools Remediator does post-remediation evaluation (PRE); independent IEP does verification (PRV)
US EPA, schools and commercial buildings Federal agency; building managers and contractors By area: < 10 ft², 10-100 ft², > 100 ft² Not recommended as routine Visual: no mold, no damage, no odour; sampling optional
Health Canada (2023) Federal; public, landlords, tenants Small < 1 m², medium 1-3 m², large > 3 m² "Do not use bleach"; good firms prioritize removal over sealing or chemical treatment Recommends a separate company for post-remediation clearance on structural jobs
CCA 82 Mould Guidelines (2004; reviewed 2018) Canadian Construction Association; contractors Level 1 < 1 m² (10 ft²), Level 2 1-10 m², Level 3 > 10 m²; mold in HVAC moves a job up a level. Calls the thresholds "arbitrary" and says to weigh density as well as area Detergent wipe is the default; a Health Canada DIN-registered disinfectant is optional, only after cleaning, and required only after sewage floods. Goal is "not to disinfect or sterilize" but to restore normal conditions Level 2+: health and safety professional provides QA. Level 3: inspection plus clearance air samples vs a reference area before containment comes down. Air sampling "not typically required" for assessment. Includes contractor-selection criteria
EACO Mould Abatement Guidelines (2015) Environmental Abatement Council of Ontario; Ontario abatement contractors Same three levels as CCA (10 ft² / 100 ft²), counting estimated hidden mold; thresholds "subject to professional judgment". Excludes small "lifestyle mold" (bathroom grout, cold window frames) as ordinary house cleaning. Adapted into U of T's Level I-IV program Same as CCA: detergent default, DIN disinfectant optional after cleaning, required for sewage; names household bleach as a disinfectant needing a different respirator rather than banning it Same as CCA: H&S professional QA from Level 2; Level 3 clearance by inspection, air samples vs reference area and surface samples showing minimal or no growth
  • S520 replaced square-footage rules with Conditions because a small visible patch can hide large growth in wall cavities (Aramsco summary). Public guidance (EPA, Health Canada) and the Canadian industry guidelines (CCA 82, EACO 2015) still use area, though both industry documents call their thresholds a guide subject to professional judgment, so homeowners and contractors may be working from different decision rules.
  • The canonical standard is paywalled: S520 must be bought from IICRC, so homeowners cannot read the document a contractor claims to follow (AMRT Training summary).

Tools and equipment on a job

Stage Tools What they are for
Assessment Moisture meters, thermal cameras, visual and odour inspection; air or surface samples by an IEP Find wet and hidden growth; classify Condition. Health Canada and NCCEH advise against routine air testing for homeowners
Containment 6-mil polyethylene sheeting, zip-pole walls, sealed vents and HVAC, decontamination chamber on large jobs Keep spores and dust inside the work area; S520 scales containment from source to limited to full
Negative air HEPA negative-air machines ("air scrubbers") exhausting out of the zone; manometer Pull air into the zone; machines sized by CFM = volume × target air changes per hour ÷ 60 (AMRT)
Worker protection N95 up to full-face respirators or PAPRs with P100 filters; disposable coveralls; fit testing Exposure during demolition is the highest of any phase; post-Katrina work recorded farm-level bioaerosols (Chew et al. 2006)
Removal Demolition tools; double 6-mil bags for disposal; misting to suppress dust Porous materials with growth (drywall, insulation, carpet, ceiling tiles) are cut out and bagged
Cleaning what stays HEPA vacuums, damp wiping with detergent, wire brushing, sanding; media blasting (dry ice, soda) on framing Physically take growth off wood, concrete and hard surfaces; HEPA-vacuum before and after
Drying Commercial dehumidifiers, air movers, moisture readings Materials must be dry before rebuilding or mold returns
Verification Visual inspection, moisture readings, white-glove dust test, indoor vs outdoor air samples No visible mold, dust or odour; materials dry (NCCEH 2014)
  • Company equipment lists add items the standards treat as optional or secondary. One Mississauga firm lists HEPA vacuums, air scrubbers, dehumidifiers, air movers and zip walls alongside "industrial sprayers" and "cold ULV foggers" (GTA Mold Removal).
  • The one before-and-after study of professional home jobs found large drops in airborne spores (Barnes et al. 2007).
    • Across 17 Kansas City homes remediated in 2000-02 (92 samples before, 99 after), mean counts fell from 131,687 to 1,291 spores/m³ and medians from 9,461 to 664; afterwards, indoor counts averaged 18% of outdoor. Aspergillus/Penicillium fell in 15 of 17 homes, and Stachybotrys, found in 53% of homes beforehand, also dropped.
    • Methods were not standardized but generally followed EPA guidance: fix the moisture source, remove and replace contaminated drywall, carpet and ceiling tiles, wash surfaces with soap and water, disinfect with a biocide such as bleach, and HEPA-vacuum carpet and upholstery, extending to ductwork if needed. Jobs typically took under two weeks and cost the homeowner a few thousand dollars.
    • A few homes drive the average. Mean reductions came mostly from a handful of very contaminated homes; homes with low starting counts changed little.
    • In at least 2 homes, Aspergillus/Penicillium and Stachybotrys rose after the first remediation, which the authors attribute to opening drywall and cavities; those homes got extra HEPA cleaning, and the study counted only the first clearance sample.
    • There was no control group or comparison of methods, no health outcomes, and sampling conditions before and after were "similar but not identical" across seasons.
    • The authors note that no quality standards exist for the services remediation firms sell, and that many firms guarantee indoor spores below outdoor levels.

Chemicals and add-on treatments

Treatment What standards and agencies say How it shows up in industry practice Lab evidence (see lit review)
Antimicrobial sprays and wipes S520-2024 strongly discourages spraying before physical removal and cleaning (C&R); they are pesticides, so label directions are legally binding; some US states require a pesticide applicator licence (PuroClean) Routine "treatment" step in many company sequences; Toronto firms advertise Health Canada-registered (DIN) "non-toxic" products and "never use bleach" (GTA Mold Removal) Kill on contact; do not remove allergens or most toxins (Peitzsch 2012)
Fogging (ULV, "whole-home") Not a substitute for removal; dead spores remain allergenic (Florida IAQ summary) Sold as add-on or main service; one US firm fogged a whole house with a product claimed to protect "for up to six months" (Memphis Mold Inspector); homeowner forums report crews fogging instead of scrubbing (InterNACHI forum) No controlled study of residential fogging found
Encapsulants and mold-resistant coatings Not recommended by any guideline as a substitute for removal (NCCEH 2014); EPA says do not paint over mold Applied after cleaning on framing and sheathing, often as a final step Coatings delayed regrowth on wallboard in lab tests (Price & Ahearn 1999; Krause 2006)
Ozone generators EPA: at concentrations that meet public-health standards, ozone does not effectively remove mold or other biological pollutants (EPA); S520-2024 says ozone and other devices should not be primary tools Marketed as "shock treatments" and odour removal Survivors at 40-100× safe exposure (Korzun 2008)
Hydroxyl generators, air purifiers Filtration is a supplement to source control, not remediation (EPA air cleaner guidance) Added as "extra protection" in some company sequences None found for remediation outcomes
Dry ice and soda blasting Accepted abrasive cleaning on framing where the surface tolerates it Marketed for attics and crawl spaces with claims of 99-100% spore removal (Cold Jet) Vendor case studies only; no peer-reviewed comparison found
Bleach Health Canada: do not use; EPA: not routine Many firms advertise not using it; some company blogs still recommend bleach, vinegar or peroxide for small patches (ServiceMaster) Best-supported agent on hard surfaces (Martyny 2005; Reynolds 2012)
  • The 2024 S520 revision itself signals that over-reliance on sprays was common: its biocide section was expanded specifically to discourage spraying without removal (ANSI).

Regulation, licensing and certification

In Ontario anyone can sell mold remediation; quality signals come from private certifications and Health Canada's hiring checklist, not a licence.

Jurisdiction Rule Notes
Ontario/Canada No provincial or federal mold remediation licence found A Toronto mycologist's site notes the business is unregulated, so anyone can advertise as a mold professional (Mould Facts, 2015); worker safety falls under general occupational health law

Private credentials seen on Ontario company sites: IICRC (Applied Microbial Remediation Technician, AMRT), ACAC, NAMRI, PMII and the Canadian Authority for Decontamination Specialists. These are industry bodies; none is a government licence.

Health Canada's hiring criteria (2023 guide):

  • IICRC certification, OSHA-certified training or equivalent experience
  • Prioritizes removing damaged materials over sealing or chemically treating them
  • Liability insurance that specifically covers mold removal
  • Written inspection and clean-up procedures, costs, warranties and follow-up
  • Labs accredited to ISO/IEC 17025
  • On structural jobs, suggests a separate company for post-remediation clearance

The ANSI summary of S520-2024 adds that most mold remediation firms are not correctly insured and do not know it (ANSI), which makes the insurance item more than a formality.

Institutions often impose stricter rules than homes face. The University of Toronto's Mold Control Program classifies work as Levels I-IVb, bans disposable respirators and requires misting materials before removal (U of T EHS).
